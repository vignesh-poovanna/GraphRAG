A CubeSat's communications subsystem is what turns an orbiting piece of hardware into something operators can actually talk to. It handles two directions of traffic: the uplink, which carries commands and software from a ground station up to the spacecraft, and the downlink, which carries telemetry and payload data back down. Both links share the same basic physics, but they're usually engineered separately since uplink data rates tend to be much lower than downlink rates, given how much more telemetry and payload data there typically is to send home compared to commands going up.

Frequency band choice shapes almost everything else about the radio design. UHF and VHF bands are popular on smaller CubeSats because the hardware is compact, power-efficient, and forgiving of pointing error, though the achievable data rate is modest, often in the tens of kilobits per second. S-band offers substantially higher data rates, which matters for missions with heavier payload data volumes, but it typically demands a more directional antenna and tighter pointing control to close the link reliably. Some missions carry both: a low-rate UHF link for housekeeping telemetry and commanding, and a higher-rate S-band link reserved for payload downlink passes.

Whichever band is used, the link has to close according to a link budget, which is simply the accounting of every gain and loss between the transmitter and the receiver: transmit power, antenna gains on both ends, free-space path loss over the slant range to the ground station, atmospheric losses, and the receiver's noise floor. If the link budget doesn't leave enough margin, packets get dropped or the receiver can't lock onto the signal at all, so link budget is usually one of the first calculations done during mission design and one of the first things re-checked whenever antenna hardware or ground station equipment changes.

Antenna types split along similar lines to frequency choice. Deployable monopole or dipole whip antennas are common for UHF and VHF, since they're simple, lightweight, and reasonably omnidirectional, which helps when the spacecraft's attitude isn't tightly controlled. Patch antennas are more common at S-band, offering higher gain in a compact, body-mounted form factor, though usually with a narrower beamwidth that benefits from good attitude knowledge during a pass.

During every ground contact, the downlink carries telemetry data continuously so operators can monitor spacecraft health, alongside whatever payload data has queued up since the last pass, and the uplink is used to send any new commands or software updates the mission needs to execute before the next contact window.
